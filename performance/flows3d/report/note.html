Why constant energy is enough to find a torus

The 3D flows are trained with one requirement: the energy must be constant on every image torus. A system with three degrees of freedom has three integrals, so it is fair to ask why one condition can be enough. This note gives the argument, shows the two places where it is weak, and checks both against the trained models.

Companion to the 3D progress page. Every number here is recomputed from the saved models.

The short version

  1. The map is symplectic, so every image torus is Lagrangian and has the right actions. This holds by construction, before any training.
  2. A Lagrangian torus on which H is constant is invariant under the motion. The energy condition is one function that must vanish on a whole three-dimensional surface, not one number, and the Lagrangian property turns it into invariance.
  3. If the energy is constant on every torus, the map is an action-angle map. The other integrals are then constant on each torus wherever its orbits fill it.
  4. It is weak in two cases. Where a frequency combination k·Ω vanishes identically, as in any spherical potential, several different sets of tori satisfy it equally. Where k·Ω is small, the energy responds to an error in the torus only in proportion to k·Ω, so a small energy error can hide a much larger error in the torus.
1 · Setting

The map, the tori, and the one function that matters

Phase space is \(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\) with coordinates \(z=(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})\) and symplectic form \(\omega=\sum_i dx_i\wedge dv_i\); here \(n=3\). The Hamiltonian vector field \(X_H=(\partial H/\partial\mathbf{v},\,-\partial H/\partial\mathbf{x})\) satisfies \(\omega(X_H,w)=dH(w)\) for every vector \(w\).

The model is a composition \(T=F\circ\tau\). The toy map \(\tau:(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{J})\mapsto z\) is the exact action-angle map of a solvable potential (an oscillator or the isochrone). The flow \(F\) is a composition of shears \(\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{x}+\nabla W(\mathbf{v})\) and \(\mathbf{v}\to\mathbf{v}+\nabla U(\mathbf{x})\), each exactly symplectic. So \(T\) is symplectic: \(T^{*}\omega=\sum_i d\theta_i\wedge dJ_i\).

For fixed \(\mathbf{J}\), the image of the toy torus is \(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{J}}=T(\mathbb{T}^n\times\{\mathbf{J}\})\). Everything below is a statement about one function, the target Hamiltonian written in the model's coordinates:

\[K(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{J})\;\equiv\;H\big(T(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{J})\big).\]

The training loss is the variance of \(K\) over \(\boldsymbol\theta\) on each torus, \(\big\langle (K-\langle K\rangle_{\boldsymbol\theta})^2\big\rangle_{\boldsymbol\theta}\), divided by a fixed energy scale. It vanishes exactly when \(K\) depends on \(\mathbf{J}\) alone.

2 · What holds before training

Every image torus is Lagrangian and carries the actions J

A submanifold is Lagrangian if it has dimension \(n\) and \(\omega\) vanishes on every pair of its tangent vectors. A toy torus \(\{\mathbf{J}=\text{const}\}\) is Lagrangian, because \(\sum d\theta_i\wedge dJ_i\) vanishes when \(d\mathbf{J}=0\). A symplectic map preserves \(\omega\), so \(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{J}}\) is Lagrangian too.

The actions of a torus are the loop integrals \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma_i}\mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{x}\) round its \(n\) independent loops. On the toy torus they are \(J_i\) by definition. Each shear changes \(\mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{x}\) by an exact differential (for \(\mathbf{x}\to\mathbf{x}+\nabla W(\mathbf{v})\) it adds \(d(\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla W-W)\)), and a loop integral of an exact differential is zero. So the image torus has actions \(J_i\) as well, on the loops it inherits from the toy.

Training therefore never has to learn the actions. Whatever the weights are, \(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{J}}\) is a Lagrangian torus with actions \(\mathbf{J}\). The only question is whether it is the right one.

3 · The key step

Constant energy on a Lagrangian torus makes it invariant

Lemma

Let \(\mathcal{T}\) be a Lagrangian submanifold and suppose \(H\) is constant on \(\mathcal{T}\). Then \(X_H\) is tangent to \(\mathcal{T}\), and \(\mathcal{T}\) is carried into itself by the motion.

Proof. Take any vector \(w\) tangent to \(\mathcal{T}\). Since \(H\) does not change along \(\mathcal{T}\), \(dH(w)=0\), and so \(\omega(X_H,w)=0\). So \(X_H\) is \(\omega\)-orthogonal to every tangent vector of \(\mathcal{T}\). The set of vectors with that property is the symplectic complement of the tangent space, which has dimension \(2n-n=n\). On a Lagrangian submanifold the tangent space is contained in its own complement and has the same dimension, so the two coincide. Hence \(X_H\) is tangent to \(\mathcal{T}\). A compact surface to which the vector field is everywhere tangent is invariant under its flow.

This is the answer to the counting worry. An arbitrary 3-torus on which \(H\) is constant is just some surface inside a five-dimensional energy shell, and orbits leave it. A Lagrangian one cannot be left, because the only directions \(\omega\)-orthogonal to all of it are its own. The symplectic construction supplies the two conditions that the missing integrals would otherwise have supplied.

4 · Sufficiency

Zero energy variance on every torus gives action-angle variables

Proposition

Suppose \(K(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{J})=h(\mathbf{J})\) for all \(\mathbf{J}\) in an open set. Then on the image of that set, \((\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{J})\) are action-angle variables of \(H\): each \(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{J}}\) is an invariant torus with actions \(\mathbf{J}\), and on it the angles advance uniformly at the frequencies \(\boldsymbol\Omega=\partial h/\partial\mathbf{J}\).

Proof. \(T\) is canonical, so Hamilton's equations hold in \((\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf{J})\) with Hamiltonian \(K\): \(\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}=\partial K/\partial\mathbf{J}=\partial h/\partial\mathbf{J}\) and \(\dot{\mathbf{J}}=-\partial K/\partial\boldsymbol\theta=0\).

Three things follow, and none of them uses any integral other than \(H\).

  • The angles come for free. The model's \(\boldsymbol\theta\) are true angle variables, and the frequencies are the derivative of the torus energy with respect to the labels, available by automatic differentiation. Classical torus mapping minimises the same energy variance over the coefficients of a generating function, and recovers the angles in a separate step.
  • The other integrals are constant on each torus, where its orbits fill it. Any integral \(C\) is constant along an orbit. If the frequencies on \(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{J}}\) are rationally independent, a single orbit is dense in the torus, so \(C\) is constant on all of it by continuity. Nothing about \(C\) was imposed.
  • The torus is then the true one. In an integrable potential an invariant torus with a dense orbit is the closure of that orbit, which is a level set of the integrals. Its actions are \(\mathbf{J}\), so it is the true torus with those actions.

The second and third points need the orbits to fill the torus. That is the first place the argument weakens. The second is that training never reaches zero variance, so what matters in practice is how strongly a small energy variance constrains the torus. Both come out of one calculation.

5 · Where it is weak

The energy sees an error in the torus only through k·Ω

Let \((\boldsymbol\phi,\mathbf{I})\) be exact action-angle variables of \(H\), with \(H=h(\mathbf{I})\) and \(\boldsymbol\Omega=\partial h/\partial\mathbf{I}\). Take the true torus \(\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{I}_0\) and ask which nearby surfaces the model could produce instead. They must be Lagrangian with the same actions, and every such surface is the graph of the gradient of a periodic function \(S\):

\[\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{I}_0+\frac{\partial S}{\partial\boldsymbol\phi},\qquad S(\boldsymbol\phi)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\neq 0}S_{\mathbf{k}}\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol\phi},\quad \mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^n .\]

(A graph over the torus is Lagrangian exactly when it is the graph of a closed one-form, and it has the same actions exactly when that form is exact.) So the possible errors are labelled by integer vectors \(\mathbf{k}\). The energy on the deformed torus is

\[H=h(\mathbf{I}_0)+\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\frac{\partial S}{\partial\boldsymbol\phi}+\frac12\,\frac{\partial S}{\partial\boldsymbol\phi}\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol\Omega}{\partial\mathbf{I}}\cdot\frac{\partial S}{\partial\boldsymbol\phi}+\dots\]

and to leading order its variance, set beside the true mean-square displacement of the torus in the action \(I_j\), is

\[\operatorname{Var}H=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega)^2\,|S_{\mathbf{k}}|^2,\qquad \big\langle\delta I_j^{\,2}\big\rangle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}k_j^{\,2}\,|S_{\mathbf{k}}|^2 .\]

The loss weights mode \(\mathbf{k}\) by \((\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega)^2\). The actual error in that mode is weighted by \(|\mathbf{k}|^2\). The ratio is \(|\boldsymbol\Omega|^2\cos^2\) of the angle between \(\mathbf{k}\) and \(\boldsymbol\Omega\).

Three consequences.

  • Small divisors. Driving the energy error \(e(\boldsymbol\phi)=\sum e_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol\phi}\) to zero is solving \(\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\partial S/\partial\boldsymbol\phi=e\), the homological equation of canonical perturbation theory. Its solution \(S_{\mathbf{k}}=e_{\mathbf{k}}/(i\,\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega)\) shows the amplification: a residual energy error \(e_{\mathbf{k}}\) allows a torus error \(|\mathbf{k}|\,|e_{\mathbf{k}}|/|\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega|\).
  • At an exact resonance of one torus, \(\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega(\mathbf{I}_0)=0\), the first-order term vanishes and the energy changes only at second order, by an amount of order \((\mathbf{k}\cdot\partial\boldsymbol\Omega/\partial\mathbf{I}\cdot\mathbf{k})\,|S_{\mathbf{k}}|^2\). The torus error is then of order the square root of the energy error.
  • If \(\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega\) vanishes on every torus, the energy does not depend on the action combination along \(\mathbf{k}\) at all, and the mode is invisible at every order. This is not an inaccuracy: the deformed tori are equally valid invariant tori. In a spherical potential \(h=h(J_r,L)\), so \(\Omega_3=0\) and \(\mathbf{k}=(0,0,k_3)\) is such a mode.

None of this is special to the energy loss. A torus deformed along a nearly resonant mode is itself nearly invariant: the motion is nearly tangent to it. Any criterion built on invariance of a single torus inherits the same conditioning (section 7).

6 · What the trained models show

Both weaknesses appear, at the size the calculation predicts

Near-resonance. Near its centre the isochrone is a harmonic oscillator with \(\Omega_r=2\Omega_\psi\), so the mode \(\mathbf{k}=(1,-2,0)\) has \(\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega=-\Omega_r L/\sqrt{L^2+4GMb}\), which tends to zero with \(L\). That mode moves \(L\) at full strength (\(k_2=-2\)). The prediction is that \(|L|\) should wander more than the energy on low-\(L\) tori, and that this should not go away as the fit improves.

Exact degeneracy. For a spherical target the loss cannot tell the conventional tori, on which \(L_z\) is constant, from any other family built from the same orbits. The trained models pick a different family each time: the conventional one, rotated rigidly.

7 · What can be added

Other residuals, written in terms of the same function K

The invariance residual is the gradient of K. Requiring Hamilton's flow to move each point along its torus at fixed rates \(\boldsymbol\nu\) gives the residual \(X_H-\sum_i\nu_i\,\partial z/\partial\theta_i\). Because \(T\) is canonical, in the model's own coordinates this is exactly

\[\Big(\frac{\partial K}{\partial\mathbf{J}}-\boldsymbol\nu,\;-\frac{\partial K}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}\Big).\]

So the energy loss penalises \(K-\langle K\rangle\), and the invariance loss penalises the derivatives of the same function: along the torus, and across neighbouring tori. The derivative along the torus carries the same factor \(\mathbf{k}\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega\). The derivative across tori does not, which is why the term helps, but it is information about how the error varies from torus to torus and not a new integral. In the runs so far it lowered the wander by about 20% as a fine-tune and gave mixed results from scratch.

A known integral adds a direction. If \(C\) is an integral whose value on the torus is known from its labels, \(C\circ T-c(\mathbf{J})\) is a residual that weights mode \(\mathbf{k}\) by \((\mathbf{k}\cdot\partial C/\partial\mathbf{I})^2\). Energy alone gives the single direction \(\boldsymbol\Omega\). With \(|L|\) and \(L_z\) in a spherical potential the directions are \(\boldsymbol\Omega\), \(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_2\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_3\), which span action space, so no mode is hidden and the axis is fixed. These residuals need only the Jacobian already computed for the energy.

Limits of the whole argument. It assumes the tori exist. A realistic potential is not exactly integrable: near low-order resonances it has islands and thin chaotic layers where no torus of the assumed shape exists, and a smooth map can only pass through them approximately. The loss is also evaluated on a finite set of angles (256 per torus here), so modes finer than that sampling are not constrained by the training set.
